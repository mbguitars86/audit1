# Life Priorities Product Validation Plan

## Goal

Test whether the app helps people make a clearer decision about what deserves attention next. The test is not whether users like the colors or enjoy taking quizzes. The useful question is whether the result changes or clarifies a real decision.

## First test group

Recruit 5–10 adults roughly in the intended 40s life-stage audience. A small group is enough for the first round because the goal is to find obvious confusion and weak value before scaling research.

Try to include a mix of people with different work, family, financial, and lifestyle situations. Avoid coaching them through the app unless they are genuinely stuck.

## Test protocol

Give each tester the same short instruction:

> Complete the assessment as if you were deciding what deserves more of your attention over the next 30 days. Think aloud when something is confusing. When you reach the results, explain what you believe the app is telling you before taking any action.

Observe rather than explain. Record where the tester pauses, rereads, backtracks, questions a score, or ignores a recommendation.

## Tasks to observe

1. Start and complete the 24-question assessment.
2. Explain the meaning of Priority Balance in their own words.
3. Explain why their number-one action priority is ranked first.
4. Decide whether the recommended action feels specific enough to do.
5. Save the result.
6. Download the report.
7. After a later retest, compare two saved assessments and explain what changed.

## Interview questions

After the test, ask:

- What result surprised you most?
- What result felt obviously correct?
- What result felt wrong or misleading?
- Was any question difficult to answer because the wording was unclear?
- Did the difference between importance, attention, and satisfaction make sense?
- Did the Top 3 priorities feel useful or merely interesting?
- Which recommendation would you actually do in the next seven days?
- What would make you come back and retake this in 30 or 90 days?
- Would you trust the comparison view enough to use it to judge progress?
- What information did you expect to see that was missing?

Do not lead with pricing in the first round. First prove that the product creates a useful decision.

## Success thresholds for the first round

Treat these as working thresholds, not sacred mathematics:

- At least 80% complete the assessment without help.
- At least 80% can explain Priority Balance correctly after seeing the result.
- At least 70% say one of the Top 3 priorities matches a real concern.
- At least 60% identify a recommended action they would realistically attempt.
- Fewer than 20% misunderstand the Action Priority score as a judgment of personal worth or moral importance.
- At least half say they would retake the assessment after a meaningful interval.

If the app misses these thresholds, fix comprehension and usefulness before adding accounts, subscriptions, AI chat, or other expensive ornaments.

## Observation log

For each tester, record:

| Field | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| Tester ID | Use a simple anonymous label |
| Completion time | Minutes |
| Needed help? | Yes / No and where |
| Confusing questions | Exact question or screen |
| Misunderstood score | Which score and how |
| Most useful insight | Their words |
| Least useful insight | Their words |
| Action they would take | Specific action |
| Would retake? | Yes / No / Maybe and why |
| Product changes suggested | Short notes |

## Decision after 5–10 tests

Sort findings into three buckets:

1. **Must fix before more testing**: confusing wording, broken flows, misleading scores, recommendations that feel generic.
2. **Worth testing next**: recurring requests that may improve usefulness.
3. **Ignore for now**: one-off preferences, cosmetic wishes, and features that do not improve the core decision.

The next development cycle should be based on repeated behavior from testers, not on whichever feature sounds most impressive in a planning session.
